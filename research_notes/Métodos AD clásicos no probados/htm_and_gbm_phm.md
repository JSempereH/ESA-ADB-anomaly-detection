# HTM (Hierarchical Temporal Memory) and Feature-Engineering + GBM (PHM/Kaggle-style) Approaches

## Topic 1: Numenta's HTM — What is it, is it maintained, is it feasible here?

### Takeaway
HTM is a genuinely online/incremental biologically-inspired sequence-memory algorithm with a well-documented anomaly-likelihood scoring mechanism (the basis of NAB), and the community fork `htm.core` appears to still be the "actively developed" successor to the archived NuPIC — but evidence of it being feasible for this project's 47-channel multivariate setting is weak: HTM was designed and is practically limited to small numbers of fields per model (community guidance says "~5 or fewer, ideally 2-4" per model, with "less than 10" as a hard practical ceiling), it has no documented validation on spacecraft/satellite telemetry beyond one preliminary Spanish-language thesis, and installation/packaging remains a known pain point (no clean pip wheel; build-from-source required). This is a high-effort, high-uncertainty path, not a few-hours win.

### Cited Findings
- HTM's raw anomaly score is the fraction of currently active columns in the Temporal Memory that were not predicted at the previous time step; a score of 0 means fully predicted, near 1 means almost entirely unexpected — [Anomaly detection with HTM: Anomaly Score and Anomaly Likelihood](https://research.list-group.com/blogposts/ADTS_03.html)
- The anomaly-likelihood step models the recent distribution of raw scores (a rolling Gaussian) and computes a tail probability for the current score, because raw scores alone are too noisy for reliable detection on noisy real-world streams — [Anomaly detection with HTM blog](https://research.list-group.com/blogposts/ADTS_03.html); [Numenta forum discussion](https://discourse.numenta.org/t/question-about-htm-core-anomaly-detection/8678)
- The foundational algorithm/scoring paper is Ahmad, Lavin, Purdy & Agha (2017), "Unsupervised real-time anomaly detection for streaming data," *Neurocomputing* (DOI 10.1016/j.neucom.2017.04.070), introducing the HTM-based anomaly-likelihood method evaluated on the Numenta Anomaly Benchmark (NAB); on the NAB leaderboard the HTM "AL" entry scores 70.1 vs. CAD-OSE 69.9 and nab-comportex 64.6 — [paperswithcode summary](https://www.paperswithcode.com/paper/unsupervised-real-time-anomaly-detection-for)
- Numenta's own NuPIC repositories are explicitly in maintenance mode (minor releases / critical-bug-fixes only), and the `nupic-legacy` repo was archived (read-only) by its owner on 1 Sep 2023 — [Numenta/NuPIC maintenance status via search summary](https://discourse.numenta.org/t/opinion-nupic-is-an-abandoned-project/9056)
- The community fork `htm.core` (github.com/htm-community/htm.core) describes itself on its project page as an "Actively developed C++ core library (Numenta's NuPIC repos are in archive mode)," lists 7,741 commits, 127 open issues, and 32 open pull requests, and supports Python 3.9+ (tested up to 3.11.1/3.12.4/3.13) via pybind11 bindings over a C++17 core, cross-platform (Windows/Linux/OSX) — [htm-community/htm.core GitHub page](https://github.com/htm-community/htm.core)
- An ecosystem-tracking aggregator snapshot shows htm.core's last push as 2024-02-28, and a Numenta forum sidebar references a "Htm.core and Python 3.11 compatibility" discussion dated November 2024, suggesting at least intermittent activity into 2024, but no confirmed 2025/2026 releases or packaged PyPI distribution were found — [Ecosyste.ms listing](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/955726); [Numenta forum status thread](https://discourse.numenta.org/t/status-of-htm-core/8384)
- A Numenta maintainer explained that producing a pip package is a significant undertaking because "we would have to do builds for every combination of python version, each version of C++ and each platform" — implying installation still generally requires building from source — [Numenta forum: HTM code in Python3](https://discourse.numenta.org/t/htm-code-in-python3/7763)
- On genuinely multivariate use: community guidance states a single HTM/NuPIC model should be kept to "roughly 5 or fewer fields, ideally the 2-4 least noisy ones," because extra fields overwhelm the spatial pooler's representational capacity; one user (2018) reported that in practice "less than 10" fields works, with swarm/parameter-search time growing enormously per added field; the recommended pattern for many channels is **separate per-channel/per-attribute models** combined downstream (e.g., flagging when a set proportion, e.g. 15%, of attributes show high anomaly likelihood simultaneously), not a single 47-dimensional HTM model — [Numenta forum: Can HTM be used on multivariate time series problems?](https://discourse.numenta.org/t/can-htm-be-used-on-multivarient-time-series-problems/3316)
- The only identified application of HTM to spacecraft telemetry is a preliminary study (Spanish-language, Universidad Nacional de La Plata, catalogued on SEDICI) applying HTM to an ESA satellite telemetry dataset, showing it can flag anomalous patterns across multiple channels and arguing it is promising for onboard deployment because of its low computational footprint — the study is explicitly described as preliminary — [SEDICI record](https://sedici.unlp.edu.ar/handle/10915/196172)
- No English-language HTM paper benchmarking directly against LSTM/TCN/Transformer baselines on the standard NASA SMAP/MSL spacecraft telemetry benchmarks, nor any result on the ESA-ADB benchmark itself (Kotowski et al., arXiv:2406.17826, 2024 — the same benchmark this project uses), was found — [search summary noting this gap]
- The closest controlled head-to-head comparison against a deep baseline is Castellanos-Alvarez et al. (arXiv:2102.11450, "Neuroscience-Inspired Algorithms for the Predictive Maintenance of Manufacturing Systems," on 3D-printer sensor data, not spacecraft): HTM with "hierarchical distance" (HD) anomaly likelihood scored 63.03 (Standard NAB profile) vs. 64.76 for an LSTM baseline; HTM+HD beat the LSTM on the Low-FN profile (73.18 vs. 71.43) but lost on Low-FP (42.23 vs. 51.34); critically, HTM+HD ran in 5,813 seconds vs. 16,414 seconds for the LSTM on the same hardware — roughly 2.8x faster — [arXiv:2102.11450](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.11450)
- Numenta-forum anecdotal (non-controlled) reports describe HTM as "way faster" than an equal-size LSTM and better than plain/recurrent autoencoders for anomaly detection, but weaker than LSTMs at "inference on known patterns absent from training" and with weaker long-term memory — [Numenta forum: HTM and Deep learning](https://discourse.numenta.org/t/htm-and-deep-learning/4470)
- A 2025 paper (Bandyopadhyay, Bose & Chowdhury, "A Hybrid Framework for Real-Time Data Drift and Anomaly Identification Using Hierarchical Temporal Memory and Statistical Tests," arXiv:2504.18599, published in *Int'l J. of Mathematical, Engineering and Management Sciences*, 2025) shows HTM is still an active research topic in 2025: it claims HTM learns "online with streaming data" with no separate train/test cycle and "eliminates frequent retraining," and reports the method "outperforms conventional drift detection techniques" (KS-test, Wasserstein distance, PSI) in accuracy/adaptability/computational efficiency — but gives no numerical results in the abstract, doesn't specify the application domain beyond a passing mention of "Telecom," doesn't state the channel count for its multidimensional variant, and its multidimensional extension combines per-dimension HTM columns through a neural network in what the authors call a "supervised scenario" — undermining a purely label-free online claim for the multivariate case — [arXiv:2504.18599](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18599)
- NAB itself (the standard HTM benchmark) was designed to compare HTM against other streaming/statistical detectors (e.g., Etsy Skyline, Twitter ADVec), not against RNN/LSTM/Transformer deep baselines, per Numenta forum discussion — meaning most "HTM wins" claims come from a benchmark not designed to stress-test it against the deep contamination-robust baselines already implemented in this project — [Numenta forum: HTM benchmarks?](https://discourse.numenta.org/t/htm-benchmarks/749)

### Inferences
- HTM is architecturally and philosophically a poor match for this project's 47-channel setting: the community's own practical guidance (≤5, ideally 2-4 fields per model, "<10" as an upper bound) means a single multivariate HTM model over 47 channels is not advisable; the realistic HTM design would be ~47 independent single/few-channel HTM models plus a downstream fusion rule — this is a materially larger implementation than "drop in a library," closer in complexity to re-deriving a new ensemble member than reusing an existing one.
- The one documented spacecraft-telemetry application (SEDICI preliminary thesis) and the one 2025 drift-detection paper both stop short of rigorous benchmarking against deep baselines on a protocol resembling this project's walk-forward, zero-label setup — there is no evidence HTM would outperform (or even match) the already-validated LOE/USDR/CRTF/RoCA/MemStream/GDN/Anomaly-Transformer/Kalman baselines.
- HTM's genuine advantages (true online incremental learning with no retraining, CPU-friendly, documented ~2.8x faster runtime than an LSTM baseline in one controlled comparison) are real and align well with the project's walk-forward, no-batch-retraining protocol in principle — but the packaging/installation friction (no pip wheel, build-from-source, Python-version compatibility churn) and the per-channel-model architecture needed for 47 channels make a faithful implementation a multi-day, not multi-hour, effort.

### Gaps
- No quantitative benchmark of HTM vs. this project's specific baselines (LOE, USDR, CRTF, RoCA, MemStream, GDN, Online Isolation Forest, Anomaly Transformer, Kalman local-trend) exists in the literature found.
- No evidence was found of HTM being run on the ESA-ADB benchmark specifically, or on NASA SMAP/MSL with a comparable walk-forward/zero-label protocol.
- Could not confirm htm.core's exact current (late-2025/2026) release/commit cadence beyond a Feb 2024 last-push snapshot and a Nov 2024 forum reference — direct inspection of the GitHub commit history/releases page was not completed in this research pass.
- No controlled runtime/accuracy benchmark exists for HTM at 47-channel, 18-second-resolution cadence specifically (closest proxy is the 3D-printer manufacturing dataset in arXiv:2102.11450, a different domain and channel count).

### Feasibility verdict (Topic 1)
**Not worth implementing in a few hours.** HTM is real, still has a maintained-by-community implementation (`htm.core`), and is genuinely online — but (a) its architecture does not scale cleanly to 47 channels without building ~47 separate per-channel models plus a custom fusion rule, (b) installation is non-trivial (no clean pip wheel, build-from-source), (c) there is no validated precedent on spacecraft telemetry or on a benchmark resembling ESA-ADB, and (d) the one controlled comparison found (manufacturing domain) shows HTM roughly on par with — not clearly better than — an LSTM baseline, while this project already has several deep contamination-robust models and a currently-best-performing Kalman filter outperforming on this exact data. The engineering lift (per-channel model design, fusion logic, packaging) is multi-day, and the expected-value case for beating the current best (Kalman local-trend) is weak given zero published evidence on comparable telemetry. Recommend skipping unless the paper specifically wants a "classical bio-inspired baseline" comparison point for completeness, in which case budget at least 1-2 days, not hours.

## Topic 2: Feature-Engineering + Gradient Boosting (PHM/Kaggle-style) Approaches

### Takeaway
Winning PHM Society Data Challenge and Kaggle-style predictive-maintenance solutions overwhelmingly rely on hand-crafted statistical/frequency/domain features (rolling-window statistics, FFT/STFT band energies, RMS/kurtosis/entropy, physics-informed health indicators) feeding gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost), often combined with feature-selection (LOFO importance, RFE) and dimensionality reduction (LDA, PCA) — but essentially all documented winning entries are supervised or semi-supervised (labeled failure/RUL targets), and no clean, validated "unsupervised engineered-features + GBM via self-training/pseudo-labeling" pipeline was found as a competition-winning or published spacecraft-specific approach; the closest genuine fit for this project's zero-label constraint is prediction-residual-based XGBoost (forecast-then-flag-deviation) as used in a 2024 Euclid space-telescope telemetry paper, which requires no real anomaly labels at all.

### Cited Findings
- PHM 2024 Conference Data Challenge final standings: team "goldriver" placed first (validation score 0.996590, testing score 0.999109); "PHHQ" was second on testing (0.984374); "MathWorks" was fourth on testing (0.968646); organizers noted "creative and complex approaches" were used given the difficulty of the problem, and teams were judged on creativity/presentation as well as score — [PHM 2024 Conference Data Challenge Final Scores](https://data.phmsociety.org/phm-2024-conference-data-challenge-final-scores/)
- No public write-up of goldriver's (2024 winner's) specific feature engineering or model choice was found in this research pass — winning teams' detailed methods are typically published afterward in IJPHM or presented at the PHM Society conference — [PHM Data Challenge Winners Announced](https://phmsociety.org/phm-data-challenge-winners-announced/)
- The MathWorks team's first-place 2025 PHM Data Challenge solution (jet-engine maintenance prediction) used domain/physics-informed engineered features — virtual-sensor reconstruction of missing pressures/temperatures, compressor-efficiency-drop and turbine-temperature-gradient-change features, pressure-ratio decay, thermodynamic health markers, and global HPC/HPT health indicators via a "Health Indicator Designer" tool — combined with neural networks and sequence-to-sequence LSTMs (custom near-event-weighted loss functions), NOT gradient boosting; no FFT/wavelet/rolling-statistics features were mentioned — [MathWorks blog: Winning a Predictive Maintenance Data Challenge with Engineering Expertise](https://blogs.mathworks.com/deep-learning/2025/12/16/winning-a-predictive-maintenance-data-challenge-with-engineering-expertise/)
- PHM 2023 North America final leaderboard: team "nivic" placed first (validation 472.0, testing 463.5), followed by "CUMTIIPT" and "KUL"; organizers cited techniques including multi-stage adversarial training, Xception-based multi-domain feature extraction, and CNN Bayesian optimization among entries, without attributing specifics to individual teams — [PHM 2023 Conference Data Challenge Final Scores](https://data.phmsociety.org/?p=2322)
- A 2023 arXiv review ("Machine Learning Approaches for Diagnostics and Prognostics of Industrial Systems Using Open Source Data from PHM Data Challenges: A Review," arXiv:2312.16810) surveys multiple PHM Data Challenges (PHM NA/EU/AP, various years) and documents: (a) feature types used include STFT/time-frequency features (2023 IEEE Gearbox), rolling-window statistical features (2020 PHM EU runner-up), band-pass filtering/Pearson correlation/phase-shift/RMS/kurtosis/entropy/peak-value features (2019 PHM NA); no wavelet-coefficient features were found in any reviewed challenge; (b) gradient boosting combined with engineered features appears in: 2021 PHM EU (XGBoost + LightGBM with LOFO-based feature selection, LDA dimensionality reduction, genetic-algorithm hyperparameter tuning), 2022 PHM EU (first-place team used XGBoost with feature engineering + SHAP; another team used LightGBM/XGBoost with statistical feature extraction), 2022 PHM NA (XGBoost in a baseline ensemble with SVM/Random Forest + recursive feature elimination), 2023 PHM AP (a runner-up combined XGBoost with a physics-informed rule-based polynomial model) — [arXiv:2312.16810](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.16810)
- The same review notes unsupervised/weak-supervision elements appearing in these challenges: K-means clustering (2023 PHM AP ensemble; 2020 PHM EU health-stage definition), similarity-based/KNN methods (2023 PHM AP), a semi-supervised information-maximizing GAN, a semi-supervised pipeline combining an autoencoder + XGBoost + SHAP for RUL prediction, and the 2022 PHM NA winner using "pseudo-label-based deep learning" — but these are semi-supervised (using some real labels/degradation-stage proxies), not fully label-free unsupervised pipelines, and gradient boosting in these contexts is paired with at least partial supervision, not pure self-training from an unsupervised ensemble's own detections — [arXiv:2312.16810](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.16810)
- The review's overall pattern: deep learning dominates when data is abundant (2022 PHM NA, 2023 IEEE Gearbox); tree-based GBM methods (XGBoost, Random Forest) dominate when data is small/imbalanced (2021-2022 PHM EU); similarity-based/physics-informed/rule-based methods win when data is very small (2023 PHM AP champion used a similarity-based approach, not GBM or deep learning) — [arXiv:2312.16810](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.16810)
- On the specific unsupervised self-training mechanism asked about: "UADB: Unsupervised Anomaly Detection Booster" (arXiv:2306.01997) trains a second "booster" model on pseudo-labels derived from disagreement/variance between an existing unsupervised base detector and the booster across iterative rounds — a genuine self-training scheme for unsupervised anomaly detection — but the paper's model family for the booster was not confirmed as gradient boosting in the retrieved snippet (would need full-text check) — [arXiv:2306.01997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.01997)
- "Self-supervise, Refine, Repeat" (arXiv:2106.06115) trains an ensemble of one-class classifiers on disjoint subsets, aggregates predictions into binary pseudo-labels, removes points predicted anomalous, and retrains on the cleaned data — a general unsupervised self-training/refinement pattern applicable in principle to a GBM-based detector, but not GBM-specific in the paper as retrieved — [arXiv:2106.06115](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.06115)
- No GBM-specific one-class or density-estimation anomaly method distinct from Isolation Forest was found. "Extended Isolation Forest" (Hariri, Carrasco Kind & Brunner, arXiv:1811.02141) is a refinement of Isolation Forest using random-hyperplane (not axis-aligned) splits to fix scoring artifacts/bias in the standard algorithm, with no appreciable difference in training speed or convergence versus standard Isolation Forest — this is a tree-ensemble variant already in the same family as the project's existing Isolation Forest / PyOD IForest baseline, not a genuinely distinct GBM-based density method — [arXiv:1811.02141](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.02141)
- No "GBM-based one-class" or "boosted one-class SVM" method was found in this search pass; this remains an open gap rather than a confirmed non-existence.
- 2024-2026 spacecraft-specific GBM anomaly detection: a 2024 arXiv preprint (arXiv:2411.05596) on the **Euclid space telescope** analyzes temperature telemetry from February-August 2024 (11 temperature parameters as forecast targets, 35 covariates, 46 parameters total out of the spacecraft's full telemetry set) using one XGBoost model to forecast each temperature from its own history and flag deviations from the forecast as anomalies, plus a second XGBoost model relating covariates to the flagged anomalies; this is a forecast-residual approach requiring no real anomaly labels, directly analogous to the project's already-implemented Kalman local-trend model but using XGBoost as the forecaster instead of a Kalman filter; no quantitative performance metrics, CPU/GPU details, or explicit walk-forward/streaming evaluation protocol were stated in the abstract — [arXiv:2411.05596](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2411.05596)
- Other spacecraft GBM precedents (not 2024-2026, but establish the paradigm is used in this exact domain): a 2021 paper (Li et al., WiSATS 2020 proceedings) used gradient boosting regression with recursive feature elimination as a health-baseline model validated on power-subsystem telemetry; a 2023 SpaceOps paper used LightGBM for feature selection/ranking ahead of a contrastive-learning anomaly scorer; a 2023 PHM Society paper applied LightGBM to thruster-failure detection using H-II Transfer Vehicle propulsion telemetry (simulator-based training data, i.e., supervised); a 2020 Chinese paper used LightGBM for operating-mode classification (not anomaly detection, 99.9% accuracy, supervised) on the HXMT satellite — [research.buaa.edu.cn listing](https://research.buaa.edu.cn/en/publications/satellite-telemetry-anomaly-detection-based-on-gradient-boosting-/); [SpaceOps 2023 paper](https://publications.spaceops.org/2023/download_by_id.php?id=0169); [PHM Society propulsion LightGBM paper](https://papers.phmsociety.org/index.php/phmap/article/view/3719)
- General (non-space, non-competition-attributed) corroboration that rolling statistics, FFT amplitude features, and wavelet coefficients are standard GBM inputs in predictive maintenance: an AnalyticsVidhya tutorial notes XGBoost treats inputs as independent and needs rolling/lag features for temporal context; a 2019 French conference paper (ENEDIS electricity distributor) applies wavelet-based features to predictive-maintenance event logs; these are general-practice sources, not challenge-winning write-ups — [AnalyticsVidhya: XGBoost for Time-Series Forecasting](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2024/01/xgboost-for-time-series-forecasting/); [HAL: Predictive maintenance from event logs using wavelet-based features](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01856309)

### Inferences
- The "engineered features + GBM" paradigm is real and well-documented as winning or near-winning in PHM Society challenges specifically when training data is small/imbalanced — but virtually every documented instance uses real (or at least degradation-proxy/weakly-supervised) labels for the GBM's target; none of the reviewed PHM winners run in a genuinely zero-label, pure self-training unsupervised mode.
- The most defensible way to apply this paradigm under this project's true zero-label constraint is **not** classification via pseudo-labels from the project's own ensemble (which risks circular validation — training a GBM to agree with the ensemble it's meant to complement, with no independent signal) but rather **forecast-residual anomaly scoring**: train XGBoost/LightGBM as a one-step/short-horizon forecaster per channel (or per channel-cluster) on rolling-window + FFT-band engineered features, then flag large residuals — exactly the approach used in the 2024 Euclid paper and directly parallel to the project's already-implemented, currently-best Kalman local-trend model. This requires no labels at all, fits the walk-forward online protocol naturally (refit/update the GBM incrementally on a rolling window), and is a genuinely different model family (gradient-boosted trees vs. a linear-Gaussian Kalman filter) than anything already implemented.
- A GBM forecast-residual model is unlikely to clearly beat the Kalman filter on smooth/slowly-varying channels (Kalman is near-optimal for locally-linear dynamics with Gaussian noise), but could plausibly be complementary on channels with nonlinear, regime-dependent, or multi-modal dynamics that a linear Kalman model underfits — this is a reasonable hypothesis for the paper's related-work/ablation narrative, not a proven result from any source found.
- The self-training pseudo-labeling research (UADB, "Self-supervise, Refine, Repeat") is the right citation family for the paper's related-work section on why pure self-training from an unsupervised ensemble is a known-but-risky idea (confirmation bias / error amplification), rather than something to implement — the literature does not show a clean win for this pattern in a GBM-specific tabular telemetry setting.

### Gaps
- No full-text access was obtained for the goldriver (PHM 2024 winner) or nivic (PHM 2023 winner) methodology papers — their specific feature engineering remains unknown from this pass; would need direct IJPHM/PHM-conference-proceedings lookup.
- Could not confirm whether UADB's or "Self-supervise, Refine, Repeat"'s booster/classifier model family is specifically gradient boosting (vs. neural network or other) — full-text check needed.
- No GBM-based one-class/density-estimation method distinct from (Extended) Isolation Forest was identified; this may mean no such established method exists, or simply wasn't surfaced by this search — treat as unresolved rather than confirmed absent.
- No quantitative performance numbers, CPU/GPU compute details, or explicit streaming/walk-forward evaluation protocol were available for the 2024 Euclid XGBoost paper (arXiv:2411.05596) from the abstract alone; a full-text read would be needed to assess true feasibility/parity with this project's protocol.
- No 2024-2026 paper was found applying the engineered-features+GBM paradigm to a multivariate satellite telemetry benchmark comparable to ESA-ADB (47 channels, 18s resolution, walk-forward, zero labels) specifically — the closest is the single-purpose Euclid temperature-forecasting paper (11 targets, not a general 47-channel multivariate anomaly detector).

### Feasibility verdict (Topic 2)
**Worth implementing in a few hours, but only in the forecast-residual form, not as classification-via-pseudo-labels.** Concretely: (1) engineer per-channel rolling-window statistics (mean/std/skew/kurtosis over multiple lag windows) and FFT band-energy features (a handful of frequency bins given 18s-resolution data) from each channel's own history; (2) train a LightGBM or XGBoost regressor per channel (or per small channel-cluster) to forecast the next value from these lagged engineered features, refit/update periodically within the walk-forward protocol (GBMs retrain fast, so periodic incremental refitting on a rolling window is cheap on CPU even for 47 channels); (3) score anomalies from forecast residuals, exactly as in the 2024 Euclid XGBoost paper. This needs zero labels, is CPU-fast (gradient boosting trains in seconds-to-minutes per channel on this data scale), and is a genuinely distinct model family from everything already implemented (tree-ensemble forecaster vs. deep autoencoders / Kalman filter / isolation forest). Recommend explicitly avoiding the "pseudo-label self-training classification" variant — the literature shows real competition wins require real/weak labels, and self-training purely from the project's own ensemble risks circular, inflated validation that a reviewer would likely flag. Budget: roughly 3-5 hours for feature engineering + per-channel GBM forecaster + residual scoring + walk-forward integration, which fits comfortably in a "few hours" window.
