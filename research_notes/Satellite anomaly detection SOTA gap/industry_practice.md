# Industry Practice: Operational Anomaly Detection on Satellite/Spacecraft Telemetry

## ESA/ESOC: beyond ATHMoS/ViDA, what is actually used operationally?

### Takeaway
Everything publicly documented from ESA beyond DLR's ATHMoS/ViDA is either a low-TRL research prototype (KETTY, TRL 2), a benchmark/dataset effort explicitly framed as "not yet ready for operational deployment" (ESA-ADB), or an externally-contracted tool still in pilot/validation stages with industry partners (CASTeC, reaching TRL 8 only recently and only for telecom/constellation operators, not confirmed as ESOC mission-control infrastructure). No source found describes a ML-based system as standard, agency-wide ESOC operational infrastructure replacing or supplementing limit checking across ESA missions.

### Cited Findings
- ESA's KETTY (Knowledge ExtracTion from TelemetrY) is a software prototype for automatic detection/diagnosis of anomalous or novel telemetry conditions, tested on XMM telemetry, explicitly listed at TRL 2 (early-stage), with the project page itself stating "KETTY reports still too many false alarms" and noting a follow-on GSTP study, "Context aware spacecraft telemetry checking" (which became CASTeC, see below) — [ESA project page](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Engineering_Technology/Shaping_the_Future/Automatic_behavior_detection_and_interpretation_from_low_level_data_sets_such_as_telemetry)
- The ESA Anomaly Detection Benchmark (ESA-ADB, arXiv:2406.17826), built by ESA operations engineers with ML experts on annotated telemetry from three ESA missions, concludes that widely-used anomaly detection algorithms, "even with their adaptations, are not yet suitable for effective deployment" and frames the benchmark as an open research challenge, not a deployed tool — [arXiv 2406.17826](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17826v1); [PMLR companion paper](https://data.mlr.press/assets/pdf/v03-23.pdf)
- CASTeC (Context Aware Spacecraft Telemetry Checking), developed by S.A.T.E. S.r.l. and Planetek Italia under an ESA Discovery/Connectivity & Resilience program contract, is a ground-based tool explicitly designed to go beyond fixed-threshold limit checking (argued to miss "incipient trends" and in-bound anomalies); it learns nominal behavior from historical/simulated telemetry, flags novelty/trend alerts, and prioritizes alerts for operators — [IAC-21,B6,IP,7 abstract](https://p.iafastro.directory/iac/paper/id/66143/abstract-pdf/IAC-21,B6,IP,7,x66143.brief.pdf); [Planetek project page](https://www.planetek.it/en/projects/castec_context_aware_spacecraft_telemetry_checking)
- CASTeC's ESA project page states the software reached TRL 8 through "validation in an operational scenario" with external "alpha" customers, and that "the software is ready for commercialization" — but the page header still lists overall project status as "Ongoing" (dated 19/07/2024), and no specific ESA mission or ESOC integration is named; validation is described as being with constellation operators (commercial), not confirmed as ESOC infrastructure — [ESA Resilience project page](https://resilience.esa.int/projects/castec)
- CASTeC's method explicitly depends on "historical data" to characterize expected satellite behavior; the project page does not address how a brand-new satellite with no history would be handled.
- EUMETSAT's Sentinel-3 Flight Control Team (a real operational flight-control team, not a research group) is, as of 2025, evaluating several ML tools for novelty detection on spacecraft telemetry, with the stated **goal** of integrating at least one into "routine health monitoring" — this is evaluation/pilot stage, not yet confirmed deployed — [SpaceOps 2025 paper abstract/search summary, publications.spaceops.org id=0408](https://publications.spaceops.org/2025/download_by_id.php?id=0408)
- The same EUMETSAT work reports that long-term ageing and environmental trends make telemetry hard for conventional ML to model, producing many "novelties" that distract operators from critical issues, and that EUMETSAT is adding domain-specific knowledge to reduce false positives — a direct, named-operator statement of why pure unsupervised ML is not yet operationally trusted as-is.
- ESA's Euclid Space Telescope operations team has published work on ML-driven anomaly detection and forecasting (arXiv:2411.05596); full text could not be extracted (PDF parsing failed), so operational-deployment status for Euclid specifically is a gap in this research pass — [arXiv 2411.05596](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.05596) (unverified in detail — flagged as a gap, not a confirmed finding)
- A 2014 ESA SMOS mission operations note documents routine out-of-limit (OOL) alarm logic in actual use: a thermistor reading went negative against a hard limit set at zero, but no OOL alarm fired because the alarm logic requires two consecutive out-of-limit samples; the operations team's resolution was to redefine the limit, not to deploy any anomaly-detection algorithm — direct evidence that simple threshold/consecutive-sample alarm logic is the real operational mechanism in at least this ESA mission as of 2014 — [ESA SMOS weekly report, Jan 2014](https://ftp-qras.earth.esa.int/smos/smos_instruments/weekly/2014/smos--smos_instruments--weekly--20140120.pdf)

### Inferences
- ESA's own publicly visible trajectory (KETTY → CASTeC) shows a consistent pattern: statistical/ML-based novelty detection is pursued as an *augmentation* layer on top of existing limit-checking, explicitly motivated by limit-checking's known blind spots (in-bound anomalies, slow trends), but each successive effort has taken years to inch up the TRL scale and still lacks a public confirmation of agency-wide ESOC operational adoption across ESA's own (non-commercial) missions.
- The EUMETSAT Sentinel-3 case is probably the single best piece of evidence in this entire research area for "where the industry actually is in 2025": a real flight control team, on a real operational mission, still at the evaluation/pilot stage for ML novelty detection, citing false-positive/novelty-fatigue as the main blocker.

- Could not retrieve full text of the Euclid ML anomaly detection/forecasting paper (PDF text extraction failed); its claims about deployment status are unverified here.
- Could not retrieve full text of the SpaceOps 2025 paper describing EUMETSAT's evaluation (PDF binary, not machine-readable via available tools); only the search-engine-summarized abstract was available.
- No ESOC-wide or agency-level statement found (e.g., a SpaceOps keynote or ESA technical note) that directly addresses "what ESOC flight control teams use today, mission-by-mission, for anomaly detection" outside of the specific project pages found.

## NASA/JPL: what anomaly detection tooling is actually deployed operationally (beyond Telemanom academic papers)?

### Takeaway
The strongest confirmed case of real operational (not just research) use of a statistical/AI anomaly-detection technique in NASA spaceflight operations is NASA Ames' Inductive Monitoring System (IMS) — used on Space Shuttle missions (including analysis tied to Columbia's final flight) and deployed on ISS Mission Control consoles at JSC. By contrast, JPL's Telemanom (Hundman et al. 2018, LSTM + nonparametric dynamic thresholding) and the related MSL tool (sometimes called MARTTE) remain, per all available sources, research/evaluation-stage systems tested on SMAP/MSL telemetry — no source confirms flight-operations deployment of either.

### Cited Findings
- NASA Ames' Inductive Monitoring System (IMS) builds a nominal-behavior baseline from archived/simulated sensor data using AI/data-mining techniques and explicitly "does not require examples of anomalous behavior, or failures, to learn" — i.e., it is designed as an unsupervised, cold-start-friendly technique — [NASA Spinoff/NTRS summary via TechBriefs](https://www.techbriefs.com/component/content/article/5209-inductive-system-monitors-tasks); [NTRS 20090002491](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20090002491)
- IMS detected an anomaly in Columbia's final-flight launch telemetry shortly after the foam-impact event, and was subsequently used operationally on Shuttle missions STS-121, STS-115, and STS-116 to analyze Wing Leading Edge Impact Detection System (WLEIDS) data for possible launch-debris damage, where it "independently verified findings of the WLEIDS Mission Evaluation Room (MER) analysts and indicated additional points of interest that were subsequently investigated by the MER team" — this is a directly confirmed case of a statistical/AI anomaly-detection tool contributing to real human-spaceflight safety operations — [NASA source via TechBriefs/NTRS](https://www.techbriefs.com/component/content/article/5209-inductive-system-monitors-tasks)
- IMS was also deployed as an anomaly detection tool on ISS mission-control consoles in the JSC Mission Operations Directorate, trained to detect faults in the ISS Control Moment Gyroscope (CMG) systems, and in lab testing "detected several minor anomalies in real-time CMG data" — [NTRS/NASA Spinoff sources, circa 2008-2009](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20090002491)
- A 2017 JPL paper (Muñoz Fernández, Yue, Weber) describes an ML-based anomaly detection tool for the Mars Science Laboratory (MSL) mission, explicitly stating that flight control centers "traditionally rely on tools that can perform limit checking or simple trend analysis," and reports a 4% false-alarm rate in initial MSL testing; the paper frames the tool as adding "decision support" and does not claim operational deployment/adoption by MSL flight controllers — [JPL/SMCIT 2017 summary](https://smcit.ecs.baylor.edu/2017/p13.html); [NTRS 20210007857 preprint record](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210007857)
- Telemanom (Hundman et al., KDD 2018), using LSTM networks with nonparametric dynamic thresholding, was tested on NASA SMAP and MSL telemetry; a 2020 GSAW presentation by Constantinou et al. (co-author overlap with the Telemanom team) revisits LSTM-based anomaly detection on SMAP/MSL telemetry, framing it around the motivation that "increasing operational demands to analyze spacecraft telemetry can no longer be met with traditional approaches" — but this is evaluation on historical mission data, not a statement of operational deployment — [GSAW 2020 Constantinou slides](https://gsaw.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/2020s09_constantinou.pdf); [KDD 2018 talk](https://videolectures.net/kdd2018_hundman_detecting_spacecraft_anomalies)
- No source found in this research pass states that Telemanom (or a direct descendant) is running in JPL or NASA flight operations as the system-of-record for anomaly detection; a general review characterization found states that "spacecraft anomaly detection is mostly based on fixed-threshold alarms or out-of-limit methods," implying learned models were not the operational norm as of that review — [review summary via search, exact source not independently re-verified beyond search snippet]

### Inferences
- NASA's one clearly-confirmed operational success story (IMS) predates the modern deep-learning wave by roughly a decade and is a relatively simple inductive/statistical-deviation method, not a neural anomaly detector — suggesting that what gets into real ops is valued more for being unsupervised, interpretable (parameter-contribution breakdown), and robust with no failure examples required, than for being state-of-the-art ML.
- The repeated academic framing ("flight control centers traditionally rely on limit checking or simple trend analysis") across both the 2017 JPL paper and the Telemanom lineage, written by people close to or at JPL, is itself strong circumstantial confirmation that limit-checking + trend analysis remains the actual operational baseline at JPL as of the time each paper was written (2017–2020), with ML positioned explicitly as an unproven augmentation layer.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm IMS's current (2023-2026) operational status — all sourced material dates to roughly 2008-2009; whether it remains in active use on ISS consoles today is unknown.
- Could not find any NASA/JPL source from 2023-2026 that confirms or denies operational (flight-ops) deployment of any deep-learning-based anomaly detector; this remains an open question for JPL's current practice.
- Could not access full text of the 2017 JPL MSL paper or the GSAW slides' later pages to extract further operational detail (PDF binary not machine-readable via available tools).

- After a December 2025 incident involving Starlink-35956 (fuel tank gas release, ~4 km semi-major-axis drop), SpaceX stated it is "deploying software to its vehicles that increases protections against this type of event" — confirms SpaceX responds to anomalies with software/protection updates, but gives no detail on detection method (rule-based vs. learned) — [trade press coverage via search summary]

### Inferences
- The complete absence of operator-authored technical disclosure (as opposed to incident press statements) from any large commercial constellation operator is itself a meaningful finding: these companies treat telemetry monitoring methodology as a competitive/proprietary internals topic, not something disclosed via engineering blogs or conference papers, in contrast to ESA/NASA/EUMETSAT which do publish in SpaceOps/IAC/GSAW venues.

## Is there a consistent industry answer for "day 1" / cold-start handling (zero anomaly history)?

### Takeaway
No single documented industry standard was found, but the available sources converge on a consistent informal answer: new satellites are protected at launch by pre-set static engineering limits (from component datasheets, qualification/test envelopes, and flight rules) rather than by any learned/data-driven model, and data-driven anomaly detection is explicitly acknowledged in the literature as being handicapped by "insufficient training data in initial operation" — with proposed (but not confirmed-deployed) mitigations being cross-satellite/fleet health indices, ground-test-seeded baselines, and techniques designed from the outset to need no historical anomaly examples (e.g., NASA's IMS).

### Cited Findings
- A review-style source states plainly: "The most primitive anomaly detection method in satellites is limit checking, in which telemetry values are checked to determine whether they are within a predefined range. However, the complexity of current satellite systems and large number of onboard sensors make it difficult to set appropriate thresholds for all telemetry, and some anomalies occur within threshold values" — confirming limit-checking (set pre-launch from engineering limits) as the default, and its known failure mode (in-bound/incipient anomalies) [PHM Society paper, exact title/DOI not captured in search snippet — flagged]
- A Tokyo Metropolitan University research group states the cold-start gap directly: "data-driven approaches to anomaly detection have problems such as low explainability and insufficient training data in initial operation," and proposes a health index designed to be shared "across different satellites," i.e., explicitly proposing fleet/sibling-satellite data sharing as the mitigation for a new satellite's lack of history — [PHM Society paper via search summary, exact URL not independently re-fetched — flagged as secondary]
- ESA-related anomaly detection work frames the standard non-cold-start approach as "establishing a normality model based on available nominal telemetry periods and searching for significant deviations from that model," which by construction requires a satellite to already have accumulated nominal operational telemetry — implying a genuinely new satellite is outside this paradigm's design envelope until enough in-orbit data accumulates — [search summary of ESA-related work, specific paper not independently re-fetched]
- Flight/component heritage literature confirms heritage-based threshold-borrowing is common practice in spacecraft engineering generally, but also documents it as risky and informally managed: "more than 50% of NASA's space missions encountered problems linked to heritage components, and heritage technologies are assessed ad-hoc [and] no systematic methodology exists" (citing a GAO figure) — this supports that "borrow thresholds from a sister satellite/bus heritage" is indeed a real practice, but one acknowledged in the literature as unsystematic and a known risk factor, not a validated cold-start solution — [passive-components.eu / academic paper citing GAO, via search summary]
- NASA Ames' IMS is explicitly designed to sidestep the cold-start-for-failure-examples problem (it needs no anomaly examples, only a nominal-behavior baseline, which can come from simulated or archived sensor data rather than requiring in-orbit history) — this is the clearest documented example of a deliberately cold-start-tolerant design philosophy actually used in real operations (Shuttle/ISS) — [TechBriefs/NTRS, see NASA/JPL section above]

### Inferences
- Synthesizing across sources: the honest industry answer to "what do you do on day 1" is a layered one — (1) static pre-launch engineering limits/flight rules always apply from day 1 regardless of ML sophistication; (2) heritage-based threshold borrowing from sister satellites/bus designs is common but informal and acknowledged as risky; (3) genuinely data-driven/learned anomaly detection is, by the field's own admission, not useful until "enough" nominal telemetry accumulates, which is why research proposals (fleet health indices, simulation/ground-test-seeded baselines, few-shot methods) are aimed at closing this gap rather than describing an already-solved, already-deployed capability.
- No source found treats "day 1" cold start as a solved problem in practice; it is consistently described in the literature (where addressed at all) as an open limitation of data-driven methods, reinforcing that limit-checking/engineering-limits is the only method genuinely available at first light for a new spacecraft.

### Gaps
- Could not find a single authoritative, named-operator statement (e.g., an ESA or NASA flight-rules document, or a commercial operator engineering talk) that explicitly describes a day-1/commissioning-phase anomaly-detection procedure end-to-end; the picture here is assembled by inference across adjacent sources, and the report writer should treat it as a synthesized industry pattern rather than a single confirmed citation.
- Could not independently verify the exact PHM Society paper titles/DOIs for the "insufficient training data in initial operation" and "primitive anomaly detection" quotes — these came through as search-engine summaries rather than fetched primary text; flagged for the report writer as needing a confidence caveat or follow-up verification.

## Published failure/incident reports: did ML-based anomaly detection catch, or could it have caught, a real incident?

### Takeaway
The only confirmed case found of an AI/statistical anomaly-detection tool contributing to catching/cross-checking a real spacecraft safety issue is NASA Ames' IMS during Space Shuttle operations (Columbia's final-flight launch telemetry and subsequent STS missions' WLEIDS debris-impact analysis). For contrast, the most recent major commercial satellite total-loss incident reviewed (MethaneSAT, 2025) shows the opposite problem: the anomaly occurred with telemetry showing fully nominal state-of-health up to the last pass, after which all contact was lost — meaning no anomaly detection method (rule-based or ML) had any telemetry to act on, and the investigation (relying only on pre-loss data and ground test history) could not even conclusively identify a root cause.

### Cited Findings
- NASA Ames' IMS "detect[ed] an anomaly in [Space Shuttle Columbia's] launch telemetry shortly after a foam impact damaged [its] thermal protection system" during its final flight — a directly documented case of a statistical/AI tool flagging a signal connected to what became a fatal failure (STS-107) — [TechBriefs/NTRS source, see NASA/JPL section above]
- On subsequent Shuttle missions (STS-121, STS-115, STS-116), IMS was used to analyze WLEIDS data and "independently verified findings of the WLEIDS Mission Evaluation Room (MER) analysts and indicated additional points of interest that were subsequently investigated by the MER team" — confirming an AI tool caught/corroborated real safety-relevant findings in actual mission operations, post-Columbia — [same source]
- MethaneSAT (launched 2024, lost June 2025): the loss began after a ground station pass "where all communications and spacecraft state of health data were normal"; 21 days and ~400 contact attempts failed to re-establish communication; post-loss optical imagery showed the spacecraft intact but in an unexpected orientation; the investigation — unable to get any post-anomaly telemetry — narrowed the cause to either the flight avionics unit or the electrical power subsystem (candidates: a PCB failure in the bus avionics, a component/circuit failure at a bus interface, or a thruster-related failure) but could not establish a definitive root cause, relying instead on 15 months of prior operations data and pre-launch test history — [MethaneSAT official results summary](https://www.methanesat.org/project-updates/results-anomaly-investigation-loss-communication-methanesat); [MBIE-hosted full report](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/dmsdocument/31529-appendix-9-methanesat-anomaly-investigation-report)
- The MethaneSAT report's own conclusion (per secondary coverage) was that there was nothing the New Zealand government funding body (MBIE) could have done that would have changed the mission outcome, though communication could have been clearer — this is a programmatic/governance finding, not a technical one about detection methodology — [NewstalkZB coverage via search summary]
- A 2014 ESA SMOS operations note (see ESA section above) documents a near-miss of a different kind: a telemetry value crossed a hard limit (went negative against a zero floor) but no alarm fired because of consecutive-sample alarm logic; this was caught through routine operator review, not through any anomaly-detection algorithm, and resolved by redefining the limit — illustrating a real, documented case where threshold logic alone had a gap that could plausibly have been caught earlier by a more sophisticated (e.g., single-sample-sensitive or statistical) detector, though no counterfactual ML comparison was performed — [ESA SMOS weekly report](https://ftp-qras.earth.esa.int/smos/smos_instruments/weekly/2014/smos--smos_instruments--weekly--20140120.pdf)
- Other anomaly/failure reports surfaced (ExoMars Schiaparelli 2016/2017 Inquiry Board report, NASA IMAGE spacecraft Failure Review Board report, E-ST@R-2 CubeSat investigation, Apollo 17 telemetry-channel anomaly memo) were identified but their full texts were not retrieved/read in this research pass, so no claims about ML/anomaly-detection relevance can be made for them — flagged as gaps below.

### Inferences
- The IMS/Columbia case is a rare, genuinely load-bearing example for the broader research question: it shows that when an anomaly-detection method is run in a real operational/safety-critical context and does catch something, this gets reported as a NASA success story — the scarcity of comparable examples elsewhere (ESA, JPL, commercial) despite ~20 years of subsequent research investment is itself informative evidence that routine operational catches by advanced anomaly detection remain rare and under-published relative to the volume of research papers proposing such methods.
- The MethaneSAT case is a useful illustration of a structural limit on anomaly detection generally (not specific to ML vs. rule-based): detection of any kind requires telemetry to be flowing; a sudden total loss of downlink defeats both limit-checking and ML-based approaches equally, and post-hoc investigation in such cases is forced to rely on historical/pre-anomaly data and ground test heritage, not real-time detection at all.

### Gaps
- Full texts of the ExoMars Schiaparelli Inquiry Board report, the NASA IMAGE spacecraft Failure Review Board report, and the E-ST@R-2 CubeSat investigation were not retrieved/analyzed in this pass; any of these could plausibly contain explicit discussion of whether existing monitoring (limit-checking or otherwise) detected warning signs before the failure, and are recommended as follow-up reading if a future pass has more tool-call budget.
- No incident report was found that explicitly states "a machine-learning anomaly detector was running and failed to catch X" or "would have caught X had it been deployed" — this specific counterfactual claim, which would be the most directly relevant evidence for the research question, does not appear to exist in public sources found so far.

## Overall synthesis note for report writer

Across ESA, NASA/JPL, and the (largely silent) commercial sector, the consistent, well-supported picture is:
1. **Limit-checking / out-of-limit (OOL) monitoring plus simple trend analysis is repeatedly and explicitly confirmed, by sources from multiple organizations (ESA/SMOS operations notes, JPL/MSL papers, PHM Society literature), as the actual operational baseline.**
2. **ML/unsupervised anomaly detection is uniformly described — by the very organizations building it (ESA-ADB authors, EUMETSAT Sentinel-3 team, CASTeC's own TRL history) — as augmentation still in evaluation/pilot/low-TRL stages, not as having replaced or been fully integrated alongside limit-checking at scale.**
3. **The one clear exception — NASA Ames' IMS on Shuttle/ISS — is older (2000s), methodologically simpler (inductive/statistical deviation rather than deep learning), and explicitly designed to need no anomaly examples, which may be exactly why it was trusted enough to deploy.**
4. **Cold start/day-1 is not solved anywhere in the literature found; it is treated as an acknowledged open limitation of data-driven methods, with heritage-threshold-borrowing being the de facto (if informally managed and risk-flagged) real-world practice.**
5. **Commercial operators disclose essentially nothing technical about their methodology; claims about commercial AI/ML usage in telemetry monitoring should be treated as unverified absent operator-authored technical sources.**
