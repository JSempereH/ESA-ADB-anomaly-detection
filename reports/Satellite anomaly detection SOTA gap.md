# The closest prior work is yours

**Flag first, because it changes how every other finding should be read: the single closest piece of prior work to this project — a fully online, label-free, walk-forward anomaly detector evaluated on real ESA-AD Mission 1 and Mission 2 telemetry — is arXiv:2609.10017, "Structure-Aware Unsupervised Anomaly Detection for Spacecraft Telemetry with Adaptive EVT Thresholding" (submitted 9 Sep 2026), and its author list includes Javier Sempere** ([arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10017)), the same name as this repository's git user and project owner. That paper already does monthly incremental retraining across the full mission history, trains and calibrates thresholds with zero labels, and reports event-wise F0.5 of 0.700 (Mission 1) and 0.698 (Mission 2) — meaning the "online + fully unsupervised + real ESA-ADB telemetry" combination this project is built around is not an open problem being solved for the first time here, it is a problem this same research group has already published a solution to. The genuine remaining gap is narrower than "online unsupervised detection on ESA-ADB hasn't been done": 2609.10017 reports only aggregate event-wise F0.5 per mission and never isolates the "anomaly" category from "rare nominal event" category, nor does it engage with ESA-ADB's own Supplementary Table 9 finding that Mission-2 full-channel anomalies-only F0.5 is near zero (≤0.001) for its original baselines. Beyond that narrow framing question, the broader research confirms three things worth knowing before continuing: no 2024-2026 survey covers the online+unsupervised+contaminated-training intersection cleanly, real spacecraft operators still run on limit-checking with ML as an unproven pilot-stage augmentation, and classical signal-processing detectors (wavelets, spectral residual, Kalman filters) have essentially vanished from the literature as standalone multivariate competitors — they've been absorbed into deep architectures or left untested against modern baselines on real telemetry.

## A same-author paper already closed most of the gap this project targets

The prior-work search found that arXiv:2609.10017 is co-authored by Óscar Alcarria, Rafael Sánchez, **Javier Sempere**, Pablo Torrijos, Juan C. Alfaro, Juan M. Auñón, José A. Gámez, and José M. Puerta, and matches this project's design on nearly every axis that matters ([arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10017)). Its protocol is genuinely walk-forward across entire missions — "the model is trained exclusively on the previous month's data and evaluated on the subsequent month" — covering 161 monthly windows for Mission 1 and 40 for Mission 2, essentially one retrain-and-evaluate fold per month for the whole mission lifetime ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.10017v1)). Training is label-free (only MAD-based statistical filtering is applied, not ground-truth purging), and thresholding is calibrated without labels via adaptive Extreme Value Theory with hyperparameters "fixed a priori without label-based optimization" — a genuinely different threshold mechanism from an operator alarm-budget constraint, but equally label-free ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.10017v1)). It reports F0.5 = 0.700 for Mission 1 and 0.698 for Mission 2 under this chronological evaluation, compared against ESA-ADB's own static 50/50-split baselines (best all-channel F0.5 of 0.061 for M1 and 0.241 for M2) ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.10017v1)).

What it does **not** do is report an anomalies-only F0.5 broken out from rare nominal events: its results table gives only one aggregate event-wise number per mission, and although its Figures 3-4 appear to label series by category ("Anom.", "Rare", "Com. G."), no anomalies-only figure is quoted anywhere in the text ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.10017v1)). It also never cites or discusses ESA-ADB's Supplementary Table 9, which is where the original benchmark paper reported Mission-2 full-channel anomalies-only F0.5 at or below 0.001 — the specific near-zero statistic this project appears to be organized around closing ([arXiv:2406.17826](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.17826)). A direct full-text arXiv search for papers containing both "ESA-ADB" and "anomalies-only" returned zero hits, and no other citing paper was found that makes the anomalies-only subproblem its explicit headline target — not the deployability-benchmark paper arXiv:2607.07335 (fixed chronological splits, no walk-forward variant anywhere, results never broken down by category) ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.07335v1)), nor any of the roughly 30 other papers citing ESA-ADB that were checked. The honest scoping of this project's novelty claim, then, is not "online unsupervised detection on real ESA-ADB telemetry is new" — it demonstrably is not, by the same group's own prior paper — but specifically "isolating and reporting the anomalies-only event-wise metric under that already-established online/unsupervised protocol, in order to directly close the specific near-zero gap ESA-ADB itself flagged," which does appear to be a genuine, unpublished angle. Two things could not be verified and are worth resolving directly before any publication claim is finalized: whether 2609.10017's own Figures 3-4 contain a visually extractable anomalies-only number even though the text doesn't quote one (the PDF's figure content was not machine-readable in this pass), and the exact relationship between this project and that paper — same author, but possibly a different model/framework, a predecessor iteration, or a deliberate extension.

## Beyond what's implemented: drift-adaptive isolation forests are the strongest new lead, PU-learning-for-streams remains a confirmed gap

No comprehensive 2024-2026 survey covers the exact three-way intersection of online training, fully unsupervised operation, and contaminated training data; the closest is Correia et al.'s "Online Model-based Anomaly Detection in Multivariate Time Series" (arXiv:2408.03747, *Engineering Applications of AI* 138, 2024), whose own stated gaps are about benchmarking quality generally — no reliable cross-method comparison exists, public datasets are flawed, and threshold selection in most published work ignores real-world conditions — rather than contamination-robustness specifically ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03747v3)). That absence, combined with the survey's own admission that benchmarking in this space is unreliable, suggests this project's walk-forward/contaminated-stream protocol is addressing a genuinely under-served evaluation gap rather than reinventing something solved.

The most concrete *not-yet-covered* method family is a line of drift-adaptive isolation-forest ensembles — IForestASD, EiForestASD, BWOAIF, and culminating in **ADDAEIL** (Li, Nair, Wang, *Algorithms* 18(6):359, June 2025) — which pairs a drift detector with an isolation-forest ensemble that swaps out degraded trees according to estimated drift intensity, making it genuinely incremental by construction rather than a periodic offline refit ([DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/083d0cf574b5470180ff9e723d5255f6)). This is mechanistically distinct from the Online Isolation Forest (Leveni et al., ICML 2024) already in this project's scope, since it is ensemble-replacement triggered by explicit drift detection rather than single-tree online structural updates. Its real-telemetry validation status could not be confirmed from available abstracts, and — notably — nobody appears to have cross-benchmarked this isolation-forest-drift family against the deep contamination-robust methods (LOE, CRTF, RoCA, USDR, MemStream) this project already implements; these two literatures appear entirely disconnected from each other.

A 2025 deep positive-unlabeled (PU) learning method for contaminated-unlabeled anomaly detection (Takahashi et al., arXiv:2405.18929) is conceptually the most on-point framing for "contaminated unlabeled training pool" available, but its own authors explicitly leave time-series data as future work — as of February 2025 it has never been applied to time series at all, confirming rather than closing this gap ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.18929)). Active learning for streaming anomaly detection, by contrast, is a real and active 2024-2025 line (an IJCAI 2025 sparse active online learning paper reports a 14% detection-error reduction versus five baselines) ([IJCAI 2025](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2025/305)), but it reintroduces label queries and so falls outside a "zero labels anywhere" constraint by design, not by omission. On thresholding specifically, label-free online calibration is comparatively mature — Siffer et al.'s SPOT/DSPOT (KDD 2017) remains the baseline, extended by Sun et al.'s confidence-sequence method (ICML 2024) — making threshold calibration a more solved sub-problem than contamination-robust detection itself ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/sun24h.html)). Finally, the general-purpose benchmark infrastructure that does exist for this field, TSB-AD (NeurIPS 2024 Datasets & Benchmarks, 1,070 series across 40 datasets), explicitly pre-splits each series into an all-normal training portion and a labeled test portion — the opposite of a contaminated-training-stream setting — and shows no evidence of an online evaluation mode, confirming that no shared-protocol benchmark currently covers this project's exact evaluation design ([NeurIPS poster](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/poster/97690)).

## Real operators still run on limit-checking; ML is a pilot-stage layer, not infrastructure

Every organization examined — ESA, NASA/JPL, and the commercial sector — converges on the same operational picture, and it is less advanced than the research literature's pace would suggest. ESA's own documented trajectory runs from KETTY (TRL 2, explicitly noted as producing "too many false alarms") through CASTeC, a contracted tool that reached TRL 8 only through validation with external commercial "alpha" customers, with no ESOC mission-control integration confirmed for ESA's own missions ([ESA Resilience project page](https://resilience.esa.int/projects/castec)). EUMETSAT's Sentinel-3 Flight Control Team — a real, named, operational team — is, as of 2025, still *evaluating* ML novelty-detection tools with the explicit goal (not yet achieved) of folding one into routine health monitoring, and reports that long-term ageing and environmental drift produce so many false "novelties" that domain-specific knowledge has to be layered in just to keep operators from tuning the system out ([SpaceOps 2025 search summary](https://publications.spaceops.org/2025/download_by_id.php?id=0408)). A 2014 ESA SMOS operations note is a small but telling data point in the other direction: a thermistor reading crossed a hard zero-floor limit and no alarm fired, because the alarm logic required two consecutive out-of-limit samples — the fix applied was redefining the limit, not deploying any anomaly-detection algorithm, confirming that simple threshold-and-consecutive-sample logic is the actual mechanism in use, gaps and all ([ESA SMOS weekly report](https://ftp-qras.earth.esa.int/smos/smos_instruments/weekly/2014/smos--smos_instruments--weekly--20140120.pdf)).

NASA's strongest counter-example is also its oldest: Ames' Inductive Monitoring System (IMS), a statistical (not deep-learning) nominal-behavior baseline method explicitly designed to need no failure examples, detected an anomaly signal in Space Shuttle Columbia's final-flight launch telemetry and was subsequently used operationally on STS-121/115/116 to cross-check debris-impact analysis, and was deployed on ISS mission-control consoles for Control Moment Gyroscope fault detection ([NASA/NTRS via TechBriefs](https://www.techbriefs.com/component/content/article/5209-inductive-system-monitors-tasks)). That this remains the single clearest documented case of an AI/statistical tool contributing to a real spaceflight safety decision — roughly two decades old and deliberately simple rather than state-of-the-art — is itself the finding: what earns operational trust is being unsupervised and needing no anomaly examples, not being the most sophisticated model available. JPL's Telemanom (Hundman et al., KDD 2018) and its MSL-mission successor remain, per every source found, research/evaluation-stage tools tested on historical SMAP/MSL data, with no confirmation of flight-operations deployment; a 2017 JPL paper frames its own tool as "decision support" while stating flight control centers "traditionally rely on tools that can perform limit checking or simple trend analysis" — an admission, from people close to JPL operations, that limit-checking remained the real baseline as late as 2017-2020 ([JPL/SMCIT 2017](https://smcit.ecs.baylor.edu/2017/p13.html)). Commercial operators (SpaceX/Starlink, Planet Labs, ICEYE, OneWeb, Spire) disclose essentially nothing technical: no engineering blog post, conference paper, or technical disclosure describing an internal telemetry anomaly-detection pipeline was found for any of them, and public information about their anomaly handling comes entirely from post-incident trade press, not engineering sources — meaning any claim about commercial-operator AI/ML usage should be treated as unverified marketing until an operator publishes otherwise. Cold-start handling is similarly unsolved anywhere in the literature: new satellites are protected by static pre-launch engineering limits and informal heritage-threshold borrowing (a practice the literature itself flags as risky — over half of NASA missions have encountered problems linked to heritage components), not by any data-driven model, since the field's own framing is that data-driven detection requires an already-accumulated nominal-telemetry baseline it cannot have on day one.

## Classical signal-processing methods have been absorbed into deep nets, not tested as rivals to them

The evidence on wavelets, spectral-residual, and Kalman-filter methods as standalone generic multivariate detectors is thin in a specific and informative way: these techniques have not disappeared from the 2024-2026 literature, but they have stopped appearing as *standalone* competitors to deep learning and instead show up almost exclusively as feature-extraction front-ends bolted onto deep architectures. FreqWave-TranDuD (IEEE Access, 2025) combines FFT and discrete wavelet transform features with an LSTM encoder-decoder; TWavefussion pairs wavelets with a diffusion-transformer; a 2022 Transactions on Instrumentation and Measurement paper wraps wavelet multiscale decomposition into a graph autoencoder — in every case, the wavelet component is a preprocessing trick inside a trained deep model, not a competitive lightweight alternative being benchmarked on its own ([OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109%2FACCESS.2025.3557571)). The spectral-residual lineage from Microsoft's SR-CNN (arXiv:1906.03821) remains fundamentally univariate and per-channel in both its original form and its production implementation (ML.NET's `SrCnnAnomalyEstimator` explicitly takes a single-value-type input column, and a 2024 CRAN package still implements it as single-series only) — no 2024-2026 paper was found that extends SR-CNN itself to natively model cross-channel structure; the "multivariate frequency-domain" papers that do exist (FilterNet, FilterTS) replace the classical SR recipe entirely with learnable filtering layers rather than extending it ([arXiv:1906.03821](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.03821)). Kalman/state-space methods show the same pattern at a further extreme: BSSAD (arXiv:2301.13031) hybridizes particle/ensemble Kalman filters with trained RNN/autoencoder components rather than using state-space estimation alone, and despite extensive searching, no 2024-2026 paper was found presenting a pure, closed-form, gradient-free, online Kalman or state-space detector as a standalone generic multivariate baseline benchmarked against modern deep methods ([arXiv:2301.13031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13031)).

This is not, however, evidence that classical methods are worse — the opposite thread in the literature is one of the more striking findings of this research. Rewicki, Denzler & Niebling (*Applied Sciences* 2023, arXiv:2212.11080) compared three classical and three deep unsupervised methods and concluded classical methods *generally outperform* deep learning across a range of anomaly types ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.11080)), and a separate study, "Do Deep Neural Networks Contribute to Multivariate Time Series Anomaly Detection?" (arXiv:2204.01637), benchmarked sixteen conventional/ML/deep methods across five real-world datasets and found no family consistently outperforms the others ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.01637)). Neither paper is specifically about wavelets, spectral residual, or Kalman filters, and neither is confirmed to use real industrial/spacecraft telemetry rather than standard academic benchmarks — but together they undercut any assumption that deep learning is a safe default, and they strengthen the case for including classical baselines in a serious evaluation. Notably, ESA-ADB's own published baseline suite — PCC, HBOS, isolation forest, KNN, global-std threshold (STD3/STD5), DC-VAE-ESA, and Telemanom variants — contains **no wavelet, spectral-residual, or Kalman-filter/state-space baseline at all** ([arXiv:2406.17826](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.17826)). A per-channel local-level/local-trend Kalman filter — the textbook "structural time series model," a closed-form recursive two-parameter filter known since Harvey (1989) and even patented in 2016 for anomaly flagging (US20160062950A1) — would therefore be a low-risk, well-grounded addition: it is old, uncontroversial statistical theory, but it is absent both from ESA-ADB's baseline set and from every 2024-2026 benchmark found that pits classical spectral/state-space methods against modern deep detectors on real telemetry. It would differ meaningfully from a global-std baseline (adaptive, time-varying level vs. fixed global statistics) and from a ridge forecaster (univariate per-channel dynamics vs. cross-channel linear structure), filling a genuinely distinct point in the baseline design space — though no empirical result anywhere quantifies how much it would actually catch, so its real contribution can only be established by running it, not by citing prior results.

## Conclusion

The most consequential finding here is not about method novelty but about the shape of the research landscape this project sits in: a paper matching this project's protocol almost exactly already exists, from the same author, which means the defensible novelty claim has to be narrowed from "online unsupervised anomaly detection on ESA-ADB" to the specific anomalies-only F0.5 decomposition that 2609.10017 leaves unreported and that ESA-ADB's own Supplementary Table 9 first flagged as near-zero. Everything else synthesized here reinforces that this narrow framing is worth pursuing precisely because the surrounding field is unusually fragmented: no survey, benchmark, or deployed system currently connects contamination-robust detection, genuine online operation, and real multivariate telemetry under one shared protocol, real operators are still years away from trusting ML over limit-checking for exactly the false-positive-fatigue reasons this project's threshold design has to confront, and classical signal-processing methods — despite credible evidence they can match or beat deep learning in general unsupervised settings — have never been properly tested as standalone multivariate rivals on telemetry like this at all. The practical next step is less a literature question and more a verification one: resolve, directly with the paper or its authors, exactly how this project relates to arXiv:2609.10017 before any claim of a first result is made public.
